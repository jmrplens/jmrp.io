<h2>About This Tool</h2>
<p>
  A 4-digit PIN has only <strong>10,000</strong> possible values. This calculator
  shows what that means against an <strong>offline</strong> attacker who has
  dumped the flash: pick a secret space, a <strong>PBKDF2</strong> work factor,
  and a GPU, and it estimates the time to sweep the whole keyspace.
</p>
<p>
  Then flip on <strong>device binding</strong> — and the offline attack becomes
  infeasible, because every guess now needs a per-chip secret that can't be
  extracted from the hardware.
</p>
<p>
  It is the interactive companion to the article{" "}
  <a href="/blog/012-device-bound-key-derivation/">
    Your 4-Digit PIN Is Fine: Device-Bound Keys on ESP32-S3
  </a>
  .
</p>

<h3>What It Computes</h3>
<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Keyspace</strong> — the alphabet size raised to the secret length.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Attacker rate</strong> — raw GPU SHA-256 throughput divided by the
    PBKDF2 work (≈ two SHA-256 compressions per iteration).
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Time</strong> — keyspace ÷ rate to try every candidate; the expected
    time to <em>find</em> the secret is roughly half of that.
  </li>
</ul>

<h3>A Note on Realism</h3>
<p>
  This models the <em>offline</em> attack on an exfiltrated vault. On the device
  itself, online guessing is stopped first by a brute-force lockout that wipes
  the vault after a handful of wrong tries — the iteration count is sized for
  unlock latency, not as the brute-force defense.
</p>

<h3>Privacy</h3>
<p>
  Everything is computed in your browser with simple arithmetic. No inputs are

</p>
