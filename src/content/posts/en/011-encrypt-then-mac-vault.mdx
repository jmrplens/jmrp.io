A password vault has one job at rest: hand back exactly the bytes you stored, or refuse. Not "probably," not "best effort" — a vault that returns *almost* your password, or that behaves differently when a file has been tampered with, is worse than no vault at all. On a microcontroller, where the encrypted files live on a flash chip an attacker can desolder and read, "refuse" has to be the default for everything that isn't provably authentic.

This post is about the file format I use in **a hardware password manager I'm building (not yet released)** — an ESP32-class device that stores credentials on its internal flash. The whole design rests on one rule that is surprisingly easy to get wrong: **never decrypt a byte you haven't authenticated first.** Get the order wrong and you've built a [padding oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padding_oracle_attack). Get it right and the vault *fails closed*.

<TLDRSummary title="TL;DR — What this format does">
- Every vault file is an **encrypt-then-MAC** envelope: `[ver][iv][hmacTag][cipher]`. The tag authenticates a **context prefix** — `ver ‖ recordType ‖ slotId ‖ generation` — ahead of the IV and ciphertext.
- On read, the tag is recomputed and compared **in constant time _before_ any decryption** — verify-before-decrypt.
- Binding slot, type, and a freshness counter makes a **relocated, retyped, or rolled-back** file fail the MAC — even against an attacker with raw flash access but no PIN.
- The freshness counters live in `meta.bin`, which is **itself authenticated**; mutations are crash-safe (append → prove → commit → index), with the `meta.bin` rename as the one commit point.
- The PIN unlocks a **random vault key** wrapped inside `meta.bin`, so a PIN change rewraps one value instead of re-encrypting every record. The price: a PIN change no longer revokes a key someone already captured.
- Encryption and authentication use **separate keys**; every reject path produces **no plaintext** and wipes its scratch.
</TLDRSummary>

<Collapsible title="Quick glossary (if crypto isn't your background)">
- **Plaintext · ciphertext**: the readable data (your password) and its scrambled, unreadable form once encrypted.
- **Encrypt · key**: scramble data with a _key_ (a secret) so only someone holding the key can reverse it. Here your PIN unlocks the key.
- **Block cipher (AES) · padding**: AES encrypts in fixed 16-byte chunks; _padding_ is the filler bytes that top up the last chunk.
- **MAC · HMAC · tag**: a tamper-evident seal. HMAC uses a key to compute a _tag_ (a short fingerprint); change a single byte and the tag stops matching.
- **IV** (initialization vector): random bytes, a fresh one per write, so encrypting the same data twice gives different results.
- **KDF · PBKDF2**: a function that "stretches" a weak secret (a PIN) into a key, deliberately slow to frustrate guessers.
- **Offline brute force**: trying every combination on your own hardware, with a stolen copy of the data and no attempt limit.
- **slot**: each numbered place where the vault stores one credential.
</Collapsible>

## Decrypt-first is a footgun

First, the threat. This is a device you can hold in your hand, and the credentials live on a flash chip soldered to the board. An attacker who steals it isn't limited to typing PINs at the screen: they can desolder the flash and read it on a bench programmer, or dump it over a debug port, and walk away with the raw ciphertext. From then on the attack is *offline* — no lockout, no rate limit, all the time in the world. The [threat model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threat_model) for the firmware treats raw flash readout as a real possibility (mitigated in production by the ESP32's own flash encryption), so the file format has to assume the attacker is holding the exact bytes it wrote and is free to modify them and feed them back.

That's what makes decrypt-first dangerous. The intuitive design is the wrong one: you have ciphertext, you want plaintext, so you decrypt — and only then, maybe, you check whether the result "looks right." To see why that order is fatal, you have to look at what happens inside.

### How does CBC padding become an oracle?

Start with the basics: AES is a *block cipher* — it only knows how to work on fixed-size chunks, 16 bytes each. Since your data almost never lands on an exact multiple of 16, the last chunk is topped up with filler bytes — the *padding* — until it fills the block. [PKCS#7](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5652#section-6.3) does this by a simple rule: if 5 bytes are missing, append five `0x05` bytes; if 11 are missing, eleven `0x0B`. When decrypting, the very last thing the algorithm does is look at that padding and check it's well-formed.

Now the part that makes it dangerous. AES in [CBC mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_cipher_mode_of_operation#Cipher_block_chaining_\(CBC\)) chains the blocks: each ciphertext block is mixed (with an XOR) into the *next* one's decryption. Think of a row of dominoes — nudge one and you move its neighbor. That hands the attacker a lever: tamper with one ciphertext block and you control, byte by byte, what comes out of the block after it — including that final padding the decryptor is about to inspect.

And here's the trap: *whether the padding is valid is something the system gives away.* If the attacker can tell a "bad padding" failure from any other — a different error message, a log line, or simply a reply that takes a hair longer — they've got an **oracle**: a box they can ask one yes/no question per try, and it always answers truthfully. It's like a chess opponent who, without meaning to, winces whenever your move helps them: that tell, repeated, is enough to reconstruct the game. With that single "is the padding valid?" bit, asked enough times, they peel the plaintext apart one byte at a time, *without ever holding the key*:

### From Lucky Thirteen to the Doom Principle

That is the [padding oracle attack](https://www.iacr.org/archive/eurocrypt2002/23320530/cbc02_e02d.pdf), first described by Serge Vaudenay in 2002, and it has been breaking real protocols for two decades: [Lucky Thirteen](https://www.ieee-security.org/TC/SP2013/papers/4977a526.pdf) against TLS in 2013, [POODLE](https://openssl-library.org/files/ssl-poodle.pdf) against SSL 3.0 in 2014.

Moxie Marlinspike distilled the lesson into [The Cryptographic Doom Principle](https://moxie.org/2011/12/13/the-cryptographic-doom-principle.html): *"if you have to perform any cryptographic operation before verifying the MAC on a message you've received, it will somehow inevitably lead to doom."* Decryption is a cryptographic operation. So the MAC check has to come first.

<Callout type="warning" title="The order is the whole game">
The dangerous version and the safe version use the *same primitives* — AES-CBC, HMAC-SHA256, a constant-time compare. The only difference is **what happens before what**. A vault that decrypts an unauthenticated file has already lost, no matter how strong the cipher is.
</Callout>

## Three ways to combine a cipher and a MAC

Encrypting solves **confidentiality** — nobody can read the secret — but not **integrity** — knowing nobody tampered with it along the way. That's the **MAC**'s job. Think of it as the _wax seal_ on an old letter: the sender stamps the seal with a signet only they hold (the key), and the recipient checks it's still intact before opening; if someone intercepted and rewrote the letter, the seal no longer matches and it's discarded unread. HMAC is that seal in cryptographic form: with a key it produces a short _tag_ from the message, and changing a single byte invalidates it.

Authenticated encryption therefore needs both pieces — a cipher (for confidentiality) and a MAC (for integrity) — but there are exactly three ways to bolt them together, and they are **not** equally safe. The canonical analysis is Bellare and Namprempre's [*Authenticated Encryption: Relations among Notions and Analysis of the Generic Composition Paradigm*](https://eprint.iacr.org/2000/025) ([J. Cryptology, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-008-9026-x)), reinforced for secure channels by Hugo Krawczyk's [*The Order of Encryption and Authentication for Protecting Communications*](https://eprint.iacr.org/2001/045).

Encrypt-then-MAC is the only one where you can authenticate the message **without touching the cipher** — the tag is computed over the ciphertext, so checking it requires no decryption. That property is exactly what kills the padding oracle: a tampered file never reaches the decrypt path. So that is what the vault uses.

Put the two orderings side by side and the doom is obvious. The composition isn't an academic taxonomy — it's a decision about *which step runs first when an attacker hands you bytes*:

## The envelope: 49 bytes of header before the AES-256-CBC ciphertext

Every encrypted record in the vault (each credential, each TOTP secret, each segment of the listing index) is one self-contained **envelope**. Picture a postal envelope: the outside carries, in the clear, just enough to handle and verify it (the version, the IV, and the tag that acts as the seal), and the encrypted letter rides inside. They all share the same fixed prefix:

The layout above is the whole envelope, but the tag authenticates a little *more* than the envelope holds. Ahead of the IV and ciphertext, the firmware prepends an 8-byte **authenticated context prefix** and [HMACs](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2104) the lot (over [SHA-256](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/180-4/upd1/final)) with the record MAC sub-key:

<Code lang="cpp" title="vault_envelope.h — the authenticated message">
```cpp
// authPrefix = ver(1) ‖ recordType(1) ‖ slotId(2, LE) ‖ generation(4, LE)
// authMsg    = authPrefix ‖ iv ‖ cipher
std::array<uint8_t, kEnvelopeContextSize> ctxBuf{};
writeEnvelopeContext(ctxBuf.data(), context);                // 8 B

// The three parts feed one multipart HMAC: no concatenation buffer exists.
VaultCrypto::hmacSha256Parts(macKey, macKeyLen, ctxBuf.data(), ctxBuf.size(),
                             iv, kIvSize, cipher, cipherLen, tagOut.data());
```
</Code>

That context prefix is **not part of the envelope**: the version byte stays `0x01` and the envelope layout doesn't change a single byte. `recordType` and `slotId` come from *where the record lives*; `generation` comes from the authenticated `meta.bin`. Credentials and TOTP entries are stored as entries of an append-only record log, a few segment files of 32 slots each, and every entry header repeats its type, slot and generation in the clear so a scan can find entries without a key. Nothing believes those clear copies: the tag, recomputed with the generation `meta.bin` holds, decides. So the tag proves that *this exact ciphertext, in this exact slot, of this exact type, at this exact revision* belongs here, untouched — not just that someone who knew the key produced some ciphertext. Why those three extra fields earn their keep is [its own section](#what-the-tag-really-binds-slot-type-and-freshness); first, the basics of writing and reading one.

The **IV** (initialization vector) is the 16 random bytes that seed CBC's chaining for the first block; without it, two records with the same first block would decrypt to the same plaintext, leaking equality. The vault draws a fresh IV from the ESP32 hardware RNG on *every* write, so saving the same password twice produces two completely different ciphertexts — an attacker reading flash can't even tell that two slots hold the same value. The IV isn't secret (it's stored in the clear, right there in the envelope), but it must be unpredictable and unique per write, and authenticating it under the tag stops anyone from quietly substituting one.

## Writing a record: encrypt, then MAC

The write path is encrypt-then-MAC in the literal order of its name. The plaintext is first serialized to a packed binary record, always exactly 4,096 bytes (more on that [later](#defense-in-depth-constant-time-padding--a-fail-closed-codec)), then encrypted with AES-256-CBC under the *encryption* sub-key, then the tag is computed over the ciphertext under the *MAC* sub-key, and finally the finished envelope is appended to the record log:

<Code lang="cpp" title="save() — the encrypt-then-MAC write path (trimmed)">
```cpp
// Encrypt-then-MAC, assembled in place as [ver][iv][hmacTag][cipher].
env[kCredVerOffset] = kCredFormatVersion;
bool ok = VaultCrypto::encrypt(keys.enc.data(), plainBuf, plainLen,
                               iv, cipher, &cipherLen);
VaultCrypto::secureWipe(plainBuf, plainLen);
if (ok) ok = computeTag(keys, id, generation, iv, cipher, cipherLen, tagBuf);
std::memcpy(tag, tagBuf.data(), kCredHmacTagSize);
VaultCrypto::secureWipe(tagBuf.data(), tagBuf.size());

// Persist: append the envelope as one record-log entry under its generation.
RecordStore::writeEnvelope(LogEntryType::Credential, id, generation,
                           env, len, offset);
VaultCrypto::secureWipe(env, kCredMaxFileSize);   // the envelope buffer too
```
</Code>

Note the wiping. The plaintext, the keys, and the scratch buffers all hold secret material, and on a device that can be powered down and probed, leaving them in RAM is a liability. Every buffer is cleared with a zeroize primitive that the compiler is not allowed to optimize away — [`mbedtls_platform_zeroize`](https://mbed-tls.readthedocs.io/projects/api/en/development/api/file/platform__util_8h/) on the device, which exists precisely because a plain `memset` can be elided as a "dead store."

## Why verify the MAC before decrypting?

Decrypting first exposes a padding oracle: the decrypt step inspects PKCS#7 padding and leaks timing or error differences an attacker can grind offline. Verifying the tag first means no attacker-controlled bytes ever reach the cipher, so a tampered file is rejected without a single byte being decrypted.

### The five-step read path

This is the section the whole format exists for. When the vault loads a credential, it does five things in a strict order, and **any** failure at **any** step returns nothing:

<Callout type="tip" title="The golden rule, in one line">
Check the seal before opening the envelope. If the tag doesn't match, the file is rejected **without decrypting a single byte** — so the attacker never gets the device to "react" to their tampered bytes, which is exactly what fed the padding oracle.
</Callout>

<Code lang="cpp" title="decodeEnvelopeStatus(): verify-before-decrypt, fail-closed (trimmed)">
```cpp
// Fail-closed: wipe the caller's record up front, so EVERY reject path
// (bad size, version, MAC, decrypt, or decode) leaves no residue behind.
out.wipe();

// 1. Structural checks: plausible size, ciphertext is a whole number of blocks.
if (len < kCredMinFileSize || len > kCredMaxFileSize) return RecordLoad::Rejected;
const size_t cipherLen = len - kCredHeaderSize;
if ((cipherLen % kAesBlockSize) != 0)                 return RecordLoad::Rejected;

// 2. The envelope is [ver][iv][storedTag][cipher]; reject an unknown version.
if (env[kCredVerOffset] != kCredFormatVersion)        return RecordLoad::Rejected;

// 3. Verify-before-decrypt: recompute the tag and compare in CONSTANT TIME.
if (!computeTag(keys, id, generation, iv, cipher, cipherLen, expectedTag))
    return RecordLoad::Unreadable;  // could not compute: says nothing about flash
if (!VaultCrypto::constantTimeEqual(expectedTag.data(), storedTag,
                                    kCredHmacTagSize)) {
    // MAC mismatch → tampered or corrupt. Wipe scratch, produce no plaintext.
    return RecordLoad::Rejected;
}

// 4. Tag verified: only now is it safe to decrypt.
VaultCrypto::decrypt(keys.enc.data(), iv, cipher, cipherLen, plainBuf, &plainLen);

// 5. Decode the packed plaintext with a strict, fail-closed codec.
return decodeCredential(plainBuf, plainLen, out) ? RecordLoad::Ok
                                                 : RecordLoad::Rejected;
```
</Code>

Two details make this safe rather than merely sequential.

### First: the constant-time compare

First, the **constant-time compare**. Imagine a combination lock that gave a little _click_ — and took a hair longer to answer — each time you got a digit right: guessing it blind would stop being hopeless, because the lock itself keeps whispering "warmer, warmer." An ordinary byte comparison leaks exactly that hint. "Constant-time" here means concrete: the comparison takes the *same amount of work regardless of the data*, so its duration tells an observer nothing about the secret. A naïve `memcmp` does the opposite — it returns the instant it finds the first differing byte. Feed it a guessed tag and the time it takes to say "no" reveals *how many leading bytes you got right*: a tag matching the first 3 bytes returns measurably later than one that's wrong at byte 0. An attacker who can measure that turns tag forgery into a byte-at-a-time search, ~256 tries per byte instead of 2<sup>256</sup> for the whole tag. The vault never uses `memcmp` on secret-dependent data; it uses a branch-free comparison that XOR-accumulates every byte difference into one value and only checks that value at the very end — same number of operations whether the tag matches on byte 0 or byte 31:

<Code lang="cpp" title="What a constant-time compare boils down to">
```cpp
// The vault calls Mbed TLS's mbedtls_ct_memcmp; this is the idea it implements:
bool constantTimeEqual(const uint8_t* a, const uint8_t* b, size_t len) {
    volatile uint8_t diff = 0;            // 'volatile' stops the optimizer
    for (size_t i = 0; i < len; i++)      // always touch EVERY byte
        diff |= a[i] ^ b[i];
    return diff == 0;                     // one check, at the very end
}
```
</Code>

The vault doesn't hand-write that loop — it calls Mbed TLS's [`mbedtls_ct_memcmp`](https://mbed-tls.readthedocs.io/projects/api/en/development/api/file/constant__time_8h/), the library's constant-time comparison primitive, which does exactly this. If you've never seen why this matters, Coda Hale's [*A Lesson In Timing Attacks*](https://web.archive.org/web/20231229151151/https://codahale.com/a-lesson-in-timing-attacks/) is the classic five-minute explanation; [BearSSL's constant-time notes](https://www.bearssl.org/constanttime.html) go deeper.

### Second: fail-closed by design

Second, **fail-closed**. The record is wiped *before* anything is read, so there is no code path — not a bad size, not a bad version, not a MAC mismatch, not a decrypt failure, not a malformed record — that can leave a partially-populated credential in the caller's buffer. The function either returns `Ok` with a complete record or a refusal with nothing. It even keeps two refusals apart: `Rejected` means the stored bytes were read and refused, `Unreadable` means the check itself could not run (a busy buffer, a crypto resource failure), which says nothing about what is on flash.

## What the tag really binds: slot, type, and freshness

A tag over `ver ‖ iv ‖ cipher` proves the ciphertext is genuine — but it says nothing about three things the format also needs to promise: *which* slot a file belongs to, *what kind* of record it is, and *how recent* it is. An attacker with raw flash write (a programmer or the debug port) but **no PIN** can turn each silence into an attack — and each maps to something mundane: dropping a letter in the **wrong mailbox** (wrong slot), **relabeling a box** so it passes for another kind (wrong type), or sliding **last month's receipt** back in as if it were today's (a stale revision):

### Binding the missing context into the tag

The fix binds the missing context *into the tag itself*. This is exactly the role of [associated data](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5116#section-2.1) in an AEAD scheme — context that must be **authenticated but not encrypted** — except here it's folded directly into the encrypt-then-MAC tag rather than passed to a single-pass cipher. The 8-byte prefix is centralized in one place, so every record module (and the key-rotation path) computes it bit-identically. `slotId` is a little-endian `uint16`, so every slot on the largest-capacity board gets its own authenticated value:

<Code lang="cpp" title="vault_envelope.h — writeEnvelopeContext (one centralized prefix)">
```cpp
// ver(1) ‖ recordType(1) ‖ slotId(2, LE) ‖ generation(4, LE)
inline size_t writeEnvelopeContext(uint8_t* out,
                                   const EnvelopeContext& context) noexcept {
    out[0] = kEnvelopeVersion;                   // 0x01, unchanged on disk
    out[1] = std::to_underlying(context.type);   // Credential / Totp / ...
    out[2] =  context.slot       & 0xFF;         // the slot this record is,
    out[3] = (context.slot >> 8) & 0xFF;         //   little-endian u16
    out[4] =  context.generation        & 0xFF;  // per-slot freshness counter,
    out[5] = (context.generation >> 8)  & 0xFF;  //   little-endian u32
    out[6] = (context.generation >> 16) & 0xFF;
    out[7] = (context.generation >> 24) & 0xFF;
    return kEnvelopeContextSize;                 // 8
}
```
</Code>

## The root of trust: authenticating `meta.bin`

Every check so far has leaned on something already being trustworthy — the keys, the freshness counters. That trust has to bottom out somewhere: in a **root of trust**, the one piece you don't get to question, because if it were forged everything built on top would inherit the lie. Here that piece is `meta.bin`.

The `generation` counters have to live somewhere, and that somewhere has to be tamper-evident: otherwise an attacker would simply lower a counter to make a stale file look current. They live in `meta.bin`, the small record the device reads first, and it is **two authenticated halves with a tag each**.

The reason for two halves is that the PIN does not produce the record keys directly. PBKDF2 turns it into a **key-encryption key** (KEK), and the KEK unwraps a random 32-byte **vault key** that every record is keyed to. The first half holds what the secret alone has to open: the KDF salt and work factor, the PIN verifier, the wrapped vault key, and a tag under a KEK-derived key. Only an operation that holds the secret may replace it.

There are two tags because there are two writers. One tag under a PIN-derived key would make every save re-run PBKDF2 (about 1.55 s on an ESP32-S3); one tag under a vault-key-derived key would mean unwrapping the vault key before anything was authenticated, a decrypt-before-verify on the wrap. Step two has to run before anything is authenticated, so the stored work factor reaches PBKDF2 unverified. The format byte does not move during development. It stays `0x01` while the layout changes, because before release the only deployment path is to erase the flash and re-provision, so a device only ever meets the current layout. Parsing is strict on that byte, so a foreign or older record fails closed and the vault is re-provisioned. There is no migration and no dual-read. (The device isn't in production, so there's no real data to migrate.)

### What a PIN change costs

Wrapping the vault key is what makes a PIN change cheap. It rewrites the 154-byte first half inside one atomic `meta.bin` write, and every record on flash stays exactly as it was. Without the wrap, a PIN change re-encrypts the whole vault, needs roughly twice the vault's space free, and so cannot complete on a nearly full device.

It has a price, and the project lists it first among its accepted risks. The vault key never changes unless you run the explicit *Re-encrypt vault* action, so **a PIN change no longer revokes a key**. A retired PIN plus any copy of `meta.bin` taken before the change unwraps the same vault key, and with it every credential written afterwards. That needs read access only, no flash write; on the hardened builds the wrap is device-bound, so it also needs the original chip. The same old `meta.bin`, written back with raw flash access, makes the retired PIN open the current vault and brings back the work factor it was derived with.

The remedy is that separate re-encrypt action: it walks every record under a new vault key, one committed step at a time so a power cut resumes it, and it is the one to use when a PIN may have leaked. It is the split LUKS and VeraCrypt make too: the passphrase wraps a volume key, and re-keying the volume is a separate, slower operation you choose.

## How do you make anti-rollback crash-safe?

Selective rollback is a [replay attack](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4949) — the attacker re-presents an authentic-but-stale file — and the standard defense is a *freshness value*: a monotonic counter the verifier expects to only ever increase, so a replayed older record fails the check. But a freshness counter is only as good as its update story. Every mutation — add, edit, **or delete** — bumps the slot's `generation`, and the record is written again under the new value. That creates a torn-write hazard: lose power *between* writing the record (under generation N+1) and writing `meta.bin` (which records N+1), and the two disagree. Resolve that wrong and you either brick a valid slot or quietly re-open the rollback you just closed.

The fix is to pick one single, indivisible moment that "counts" — like the signature on a contract: before the pen lifts nothing is binding, after it everything is. The firmware makes the **`meta.bin` swap that moment** — written to a temp file, then [atomically renamed](https://pubs.opengroup.org/onlinepubs/9699919799/functions/rename.html) into place, so a reader always sees the old `meta.bin` or the new one, never a torn half-write — and recovers deterministically around it:

An append that closed is on flash, but whether it is *live* is decided by `meta.bin` alone: a slot's live entry is the one whose generation matches the authenticated table. Lose power before the commit and the new entry carries a generation `meta.bin` never reached, so it is garbage and the slot keeps its previous record. Lose power after the commit and before the index write, and the index row is stale; the segment's own tag check catches that and the row is rebuilt from the log. Each time `meta.bin` is authenticated, the store also re-appends the committed entry on top of any orphan, so presence, counts and the compaction agree with what readers trust. Compaction copies one segment's live entries into a `.tmp` twin and renames it over the segment, so a segment is never absent. No window bricks a slot or silently re-opens rollback, and those interruptions have their own native tests. A `delete` appends a tombstone and bumps the generation in the same commit, so a deleted entry can't be resurrected by replaying its old bytes.

## The honest boundary: open vs `_secure`

Binding `generation` closes *selective* rollback — reverting one record while the rest of the vault moves on. It does **not** close a *full snapshot* rollback: revert the entire vault (`meta.bin` and every record together) to an earlier, internally consistent state, and the counters revert with it, so nothing looks out of place. On the open (developer-flashable) builds nothing detects it. The hardened `_secure` builds raise the bar without closing it: Flash Encryption and Secure Boot v2 stop an attacker from forging a new, older-looking state, but someone with physical access can still write back an encrypted image they captured from the same device earlier. True monotonic anti-rollback needs a hardware monotonic counter, and the chip's one-time eFuse bits are no place for state that changes this often.

## Does encrypt-then-MAC need separate keys?

Yes. The security argument for encrypt-then-MAC assumes the encryption key and the authentication key are *independent*; reusing one key for both jobs voids the guarantee and invites cross-protocol mischief, so the vault derives one sub-key to encrypt and another to MAC from the same vault key, each under its own domain-separation label. The PIN-derived KEK gets the same treatment for its own jobs.

It is the same instinct that says your house key, car key and mailbox key should be three different keys, and it follows the long-standing [key-separation guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/57/pt1/r5/final) — [NIST SP 800-57 Part 1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-57pt1r5.pdf) puts it plainly: "a single key should be used for only one purpose":

<Code lang="cpp" title="vault_keys — distinct labels per purpose">
```cpp
// The PIN-derived KEK opens the vault; the random vault key (VK) keys the records.
//   pinVerifier = HMAC(KEK, "kleidos-pin")              -> unlock check
//   wrapEnc     = HMAC(KEK, "kleidos-wrap-enc")         -> unwraps the VK
//   wrapMac     = HMAC(KEK, "kleidos-wrap-mac")         -> tags meta.bin's KEK half
//   encKey      = HMAC(VK,  "kleidos-enc")              -> AES-256-CBC key
//   macKey      = HMAC(VK,  "kleidos-mac" || hmacSalt)  -> record HMAC key
//   metaMac     = HMAC(VK,  "kleidos-meta-mac")         -> tags meta.bin's VK half
bool ok = deriveLabeled(vaultKey, kEncKeyLabel, out.enc.data(), out.enc.size());
ok = ok && deriveMacKey(vaultKey, hmacSalt, out.mac);  // label || hmacSalt
ok = ok && deriveLabeled(vaultKey, kMetaMacKeyLabel,
                         out.metaMac.data(), out.metaMac.size());
```
</Code>

### How do you check a PIN without storing it?

The PIN verifier is how the device checks the PIN without storing it: unlock derives the KEK from the entered PIN, computes the verifier, and compares it, again in constant time, against the stored value, once the wrap tag has already verified. A wrong PIN simply produces a different verifier, with no hint about *how* wrong it was. (The verifier sits in the first half of `meta.bin`, beside the salt; the PIN itself is never written anywhere.)

The point of the split is that each key can do *exactly one thing*. If the same key both encrypted files and authenticated them, a clever attacker could try to make ciphertext and tags interact — and the clean security proof for encrypt-then-MAC, which assumes the two keys are independent, would simply no longer apply. ## Defense in depth: constant-time padding + a fail-closed codec

Because the MAC is verified first, a tampered file never reaches the decryption code, which means the classic padding oracle is *unreachable* by construction. The padding check below is therefore **defense in depth**, not the primary defense — belt and suspenders for the case where authenticated-but-corrupt data still needs to be rejected cleanly.

The vault applies and checks [PKCS#7 padding](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5652#section-6.3) itself. AES runs through PSA with `PSA_ALG_CBC_NO_PADDING`: the firmware pads the plaintext before encrypting, and after decrypting (so only once the MAC has passed) it validates the padding in constant time and reports a single unified failure, so it leaks nothing through logs or timing regardless. The library's own CBC-PKCS7 mode is never used, which is also why [CVE-2025-59438](https://mbed-tls.readthedocs.io/en/latest/security-advisories/mbedtls-security-advisory-2025-10-invalid-padding-error/), a padding-error timing leak in that mode, does not apply to the vault. (The 32-byte vault key is wrapped with no padding at all: it is exactly two blocks.)

<Code lang="cpp" title="Constant-time PKCS#7 padding check, unified failure">
```cpp
// The last byte claims the pad length; fold every check into one mask.
const uint8_t padByte    = plainOut[cipherLen - 1];
uint8_t       padInvalid = 0;
padInvalid |= static_cast<uint8_t>(padByte == 0);             // length 0: illegal
padInvalid |= static_cast<uint8_t>(padByte > kAesBlockSize);  // > a block: illegal
for (size_t i = 0; i < kAesBlockSize; i++) {
    const uint8_t mask = (i < padByte) ? 0xFF : 0x00;         // examine the whole block
    padInvalid |= static_cast<uint8_t>((plainOut[cipherLen - 1 - i] ^ padByte) & mask);
}
if (padInvalid != 0) {   // the caller logs ONE message for corruption AND bad padding
    mbedtls_platform_zeroize(plainOut, cipherLen);
    return false;
}
*plainLen = cipherLen - padByte;
```
</Code>

The decrypted plaintext is then handed to an **in-house binary codec**, not a general-purpose parser. The decrypted bytes are the most sensitive range in the whole product, and I don't want a JSON or CBOR library — with its surface area, its allocations, its surprises — anywhere near them. The decoder is a forward-only cursor where every read is bounds-checked before it copies, every string length is rejected if it exceeds the field's compile-time cap, and any trailing garbage fails the record:

<Code lang="cpp" title="record_codec — a bounds-checked, fail-closed reader">
```cpp
// need(n): are n more bytes available, without integer-overflow wraparound?
bool need(size_t n) const noexcept { return off_ + n <= len_ && off_ + n >= off_; }

// Read a length-prefixed string: reject len > field-capacity BEFORE copying.
template <size_t N>
bool readStrBody(InplaceString<N>& dst, size_t len) noexcept {
    if (len > N || !need(len)) return false;   // fail closed, no partial copy
    std::memcpy(dst.data(), p_ + off_, len);
    dst.data()[len] = '\0';                     // NUL-terminate the destination
    dst.resyncLength();
    off_ += len;
    return true;
}
```
</Code>

<Callout type="keypoint" title="Authenticated, but still parsed defensively">
The EtM tag already proves the plaintext is genuine before the codec runs, so these checks guard a small, authenticated surface. They stay anyway: defense in depth costs almost nothing here, and a corrupt flash page or a future bug shouldn't be able to turn decoded bytes into a buffer overrun. This is the same instinct behind a real-world encrypted-vault format like [KeePass's KDBX](https://keepass.info/help/kb/kdbx.html), whose [KDBX 4 revision](https://keepass.info/help/kb/kdbx_4.html) authenticates its header and payload with HMAC-SHA256 rather than trusting the container.
</Callout>

## Why CBC+HMAC and not AES-GCM?

A fair question. The modern default for authenticated encryption is an [AEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authenticated_encryption) construction like [AES-GCM](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/38/d/final), which fuses encryption and authentication into one primitive and is harder to assemble incorrectly. If I were starting a greenfield protocol, GCM (or ChaCha20-Poly1305) would be the obvious pick.

The vault uses encrypt-then-MAC with AES-256-CBC and HMAC-SHA256 for reasons that are about this device, not about the primitives. CBC with HMAC has **no catastrophic failure mode if an IV ever repeats**: a repeated IV leaks that two records share their first blocks, not a key, while a repeated GCM nonce gives away the authentication key. AES and SHA-256 are both **hardware-accelerated** on the ESP32. And the hard part of this format, freshness, is a state problem that associated data would not solve: an AEAD would carry the same `type ‖ slot ‖ generation` in its associated data, with a riskier primitive underneath. Encrypt-then-MAC is the [generically secure composition](https://eprint.iacr.org/2000/025), so building AEAD out of CBC+HMAC this way is sound; it's how IPsec (in its standard configuration) and KDBX 4 do it too.

There used to be a third reason, byte-identical output from a host build whose unit tests ran against the legacy Mbed TLS API and a device where Mbed TLS 4.0's PSA Crypto API is the only public path. That one is gone: the host tests now link the same Mbed TLS 4 and TF-PSA-Crypto as the firmware (ESP-IDF 6.0.3 ships Mbed TLS 4.1.1 with TF-PSA-Crypto 1.1.0), so there is a single PSA backend and nothing left to keep in step.

One footnote on the implementation: PSA's transition guide suggests `psa_mac_verify` over "compute a tag, then `mbedtls_ct_memcmp`." The record path still computes and compares, with one tag function shared by the write and the read; the encrypted export streams its chunks through `psa_mac_verify`. ## How does the vault list credentials without decrypting them all?

The list index is a set of encrypted segments, `idx_NN.bin`, one per 32 credential slots, each in the exact same `[ver][iv][hmacTag][cipher]` envelope. They cache only the *non-secret* metadata a list view needs: id, name, username, brand, the domain taken from the URL, group, order, modification time and two flags (favorite, has 2FA). Never the password, the full URL, notes, or TOTP secret. Listing therefore costs one read and one decrypt per 32 slots, not one per credential.

That falls out of treating every artifact as an independent envelope. Decrypting every credential just to draw a list would be slow and would needlessly expose every secret in RAM; the index sidesteps both. Each segment binds its own number as `slotId` and, as `generation`, the sum of the credential counters of the slots it caches, so an older copy of a segment fails its tag like any rolled-back record. Splitting it also keeps the working set fixed: a single index file needed RAM in proportion to the credential count, which a board without PSRAM ran out of long before its nominal capacity.

<Callout type="info" title="One decrypt per segment">
Showing the credential list costs **one read plus one decrypt per 32 slots**, instead of one per credential. The heavy per-credential decrypt happens only when you actually open one. The index is just a cache: if a segment is missing or its MAC doesn't verify, it's rebuilt from the authenticated credential records in its slot range, which are always the source of truth.
</Callout>

## Fail closed, by construction

The cipher isn't what makes this vault trustworthy at rest — the *order of operations* is. Put plainly: never trust a file until its seal checks out, and when in doubt, hand back nothing. Authenticate the slot, type, freshness, IV, and ciphertext with a separate key; verify that tag in constant time before a single byte is decrypted; authenticate the metadata that holds the freshness counters; wipe everything on the way out; and let every error path converge on the same answer: nothing. Get those right and AES-256 is almost an implementation detail.

  rating="A"
  title="Encrypt-then-MAC, context-bound, verify-before-decrypt, fail-closed"
  description="Binding slot, type, and a freshness counter into the tag — and authenticating the metadata that holds those counters — removes the padding-oracle class and defeats relocation, type-confusion, and selective rollback before any decryption. The residual gaps are documented: full-snapshot rollback (raised but not closed on _secure builds), a PIN change that rewraps the vault key instead of revoking it, and the low entropy of a short PIN (the next post)."

There's one thing this format doesn't solve, though. All of it protects the *file* — but the key that unlocks the vault still ultimately comes from a short PIN, and a short PIN has very little entropy. *On* the device that's fine: a brute-force guard locks out and eventually wipes the vault after a handful of wrong PINs, so online guessing dies quickly. The problem is *offline*. If someone desolders the flash and copies the ciphertext, the lockout never runs — they can derive keys from PIN guesses on a GPU, with no device in the loop to stop them — and a 4-digit keyspace falls in a fraction of a second. (Production flash encryption raises that bar, but the file format shouldn't have to depend on it.) Closing that gap needs a different trick: one that mixes a secret only the original chip can produce into the key, so the exfiltrated ciphertext is useless on any other hardware. That's the [next post](/blog/012-device-bound-key-derivation/).
